## Making it

1. Best: oral rehydration sachets from a pharmacy. With clean hands and container, dissolve each sachet in the drinking water its pack says, adding nothing else.
2. If you're unsure about the water, boil it and let it cool first.
3. Keep what is left in the fridge and throw it away after 24 hours.
4. If there are no sachets: 1 litre of drinking water, 3 grams of table salt and 18 grams of sugar (WHO's home-made recipe), weighed on a kitchen scale. Never measure by eye or with spoons: more salt or more sugar can do harm.
5. With no sachets and no scale, give the drinks below and take them to a doctor.

## Other drinks

Salted soup, salted rice-cooking water and plain water also prevent dehydration. Don't give a child fizzy drinks, shop-bought fruit juice, sweetened tea or coffee.

## How much and how to give it

- After each loose stool: under 2 years, 50–100 ml (¼–½ large cup); 2 to 10 years, 100–200 ml (½–1 large cup); older children and adults, as much as they want.
- Slowly. For a child under 2, one teaspoon every 1–2 minutes (a baby, with a needle-free syringe or dropper); older ones, frequent sips. Don't use a feeding bottle.
- If they vomit, wait 5–10 minutes and carry on, more slowly: one teaspoon every 2–3 minutes.
- Keep up breastfeeding, formula (not watered down) and their usual food.

## When to get help

Take a child to a doctor if they:

- are eating or drinking poorly (a baby, if refusing feeds) or keep vomiting;
- pass many watery stools;
- have blood in their stool;
- have a fever or great thirst;
- wet fewer nappies, cry without tears or have a sunken soft spot (fontanelle) on the head;
- are very drowsy or irritable;
- aren't better in 3 days.

Call 112 if a dehydrated child or adult has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue, cold skin, fast or difficult breathing, is confused or is hard to wake.
